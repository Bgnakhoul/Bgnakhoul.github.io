Analog IC design

Design, Layout and Post-Layout Verification of a 45 nm Common-Source Amplifier

Analog IC Design (EEN 311)Nov–Dec 2024IndividualTwo IEEE-format reports

The task was to design an NMOS/PMOS common-source amplifier in a 45 nm PDK that meets a fixed specification, then take it through the flow a real analog block goes through: device characterization, corner and statistical verification, layout, physical verification, parasitic extraction and post-layout sign-off.

Design and pre-layout verification

  • Characterized the NMOS and PMOS separately with DC sweeps to extract VTH, VA, λ, k′ and gm, then sized the devices. The NMOS is W/L = 21.6 µm / 45 nm (10 fingers) biased at VGS = 650 mV for ID ≈ 1.04 mA (Vov ≈ 59 mV, gm ≈ 35 mS). The PMOS is widened to 41.6 µm to compensate for lower hole mobility.
  • Ran transient, DC and AC analyses (mid-band gain 16.6 dB, −3 dB frequency ≈ 6 GHz), then swept process corners (tt, ff, ss, fs, sf, Monte Carlo) × supply (0.95–1.10 V) × temperature (10–75 °C) in ADE Assembler. Almost every point passes; a few skewed-corner points land within 10 % of a limit.
  • Monte Carlo (200 samples): 100 % yield on gain, bandwidth, current and power.
  • Reliability: an aging simulation over a 3-year lifetime (HCI, NBTI, PBTI, plus self-heating) still meets every specification. The PMOS self-heating rise was about 4.5 °C.

Layout and physical verification

  • Built the layout with multi-finger devices and added six dummy transistors and two dummy resistors around the active devices for matching. Drain connections use Metal 1–3 via stacks and an M3 route to the output pin.
  • Cleared DRC and LVS (the layout matches the schematic).
  • Electromigration and IR drop (EMIR). Cadence EAD flagged violations on the signal nets, where J/Jmax ≥ 1 is a violation: 1.50 on net2 and 11.18 on the output. I widened the metal (net2 from 0.3 to 0.9 µm, output paths from 0.08 to 1.02 µm) and added via rows. J/Jmax dropped to 0.61 and 0.85, and the worst IR drop fell from 387 µV to 178 µV on net2 and from 8.7 mV to 1.0 mV on the output. Flags on the supply rails were left outstanding in this iteration.

Post-layout results (parasitic extraction)

Re-running the same sweeps with extracted parasitics shows what layout costs. Gain, current and power are essentially unchanged, but bandwidth falls by about 35 %.

Takeaway. The nominal post-layout bandwidth misses the 4 GHz target by under 2 %, but 42.5 % of Monte Carlo samples fall below it, and at the slow process corner all 16 supply/temperature points come in at 2.5–2.8 GHz while gain, current and power still pass. The EM fix contributes: widening the output metal roughly doubled that net's extracted capacitance (2.14 → 4.31 fF). The report's conclusion is that optimizing placement and routing is the lever; the next iteration would trade metal width against capacitance using the actual J/Jmax margin.
Embedded hardware & firmware

Bluetooth-Controlled Autonomous Delivery Car on a PIC16F877A

Microprocessor Laboratory (EEN 425)Dec 2025IndividualHardware, firmware and PC app

A small delivery robot for structured indoor spaces such as hospitals, labs and offices, where reducing human contact matters. A PC application sends a room number (1–9) over Bluetooth. The car follows a black line, counts room markers, stops at the destination, shows its status on an LCD and reports its position back to the PC.

Firmware (PIC assembly)

  • Serial link. The hardware UART runs at 9600 baud (SPBRG = 25, BRGH = 1) with continuous receive. Overrun errors are cleared by toggling CREN so the receiver cannot lock up.
  • Line following. Three IR sensors on RB0–RB2 are read as a 3-bit pattern and used as an index into a jump table through PCL: 010 straight, 011/001 steer right, 110/100 steer left, 101 room marker.
  • Room counting. Each marker decrements a remaining-rooms counter and increments the current-room state. A roughly 400 ms software delay debounces the marker so one marker is not counted twice. At zero the motors stop and the LCD shows “ARRIVED!”.
  • Actuation. Motion commands are single-byte writes to PORTC, which drives the L293N H-bridge inputs.

Hardware

  • The HC-05 module is a 3.3 V device, so the PIC's 5 V TX line reaches its RX pin through a resistor divider. The module's STATE pin drives an LED for connection debugging.
  • A 16×2 character LCD runs in 8-bit mode (data on PORTD, RS and E on PORTE, RW tied low) and shows “GOING TO ROOM n” or “ARRIVED!”.
  • Python (tkinter and pyserial) provides the room selector and shows the position the car reports back.

What went wrong, and limits

Designing a path the car could follow without drifting was the first difficulty, and early firmware double-counted or skipped room markers; iterating on the firmware, including the debounce above, resolved it. A faulty HC-05 module also caused hours of debugging through inconsistent data reception. The design depends on a clean, continuous line, has no obstacle detection, and relies on a fixed marker pattern. Wheel encoders, obstacle sensing and a more robust wireless link are the natural next steps.

Digital IC design

Transistor-Level 4-Bit Array Multiplier in 45 nm CMOS

Digital Integrated Circuits (EEN 422)May 2026Team project with Anthony El BeyrouthyCadence Virtuoso

A 4×4 array multiplier built at the transistor level, without a standard-cell library, so every gate is a sized schematic. The work covers cell design, characterization, assembly and functional test.

Cell design

  • Full adder. A 28-transistor static CMOS design. Devices are sized for symmetric rise and fall, with Wp = 3·Wn (360 nm and 120 nm, L = 45 nm) to offset the electron/hole mobility ratio of about 3.
  • AND gate. A NAND followed by an inverter, six transistors, using the same sizing rule.
  • Devices are instantiated as multi-finger transistors to reflect realistic layout geometry.

Characterization and test

  • Transient simulations of the full adder (Sum and Cout) and the AND gate, measuring rise and fall times, before assembling the array.
  • Functional tests on the full multiplier matched the expected products: 15×15 = 225, 14×7 = 98 and 11×13 = 143.

Observation: output glitches

The output waveforms show transient glitches on the upper product bits before they settle, the expected signature of a ripple-carry structure whose carries arrive late. We proposed three remedies: register P0–P7 in D flip-flops and sample after the logic has settled, replace the ripple adders with carry-lookahead, or simply wait for the outputs to settle.

HDL & computer architecture

A 26-Instruction Load/Store RISC Processor in VHDL

Microprocessors (EEN 424)Dec 2025IndividualVHDL

The course project asked for a RISC processor with its own instruction set, register file, memories and control unit, able to handle branches without hazards. My design uses 16-bit instructions over an 8-bit datapath, with a single-cycle control unit, so a branch is resolved in the same cycle it is fetched and there is no pipeline to hazard.

Design

  • Instruction word. Four 4-bit fields: opcode, a select/Rx field, destination and source. Immediate, direct-memory and branch forms reuse the low byte as an 8-bit immediate or address; JMP takes a 12-bit absolute address.
  • Datapath. A 12-bit program counter, a 16 × 8-bit register file with two read ports and one write port, a 15-operation ALU and a separate data memory.
  • Control. One combinational process decodes the opcode and select field into register-write, memory-write, ALU-select, write-back and PC-load signals. Branch targets are PC + sign-extended imm8.
  • Instruction set (26). Arithmetic, logic, shifts, unsigned compare-and-set (SLT/RLT), register moves, direct and indirect load/store, and conditional and unconditional branches (see table).

Verification

Each block has its own testbench (ALU, register file, data memory, program counter, program memory). The top-level testbench runs a program that sums 1…5 into R2 and asserts that R2 ends at 0x0F, so the check is self-contained rather than read off a waveform.

Communication systems

Bipolar Pseudoternary Line Code: Spectrum, Detection and Synchronization

Communication Systems I (EEN 344)May 2026With Habib Bou Maroun

A baseband link in MATLAB/Simulink: a custom encoder, spectrum analysis, a mid-bit-sampling receiver and a synchronization experiment, with theory compared against simulation.

  • Simulated PSD matches theory: no DC component, first null at 1/Tb = 1 kHz, main lobe 2 kHz, efficiency 1 bit/s/Hz.
  • Noiseless receiver over 1000 bits: BER = 0.
  • Synchronization test: a 0.25 Tb sampling offset still gave 0 errors, while a 0.5 Tb offset gave BER = 0.5 (500 errors in 1000 bits). The code has a transition density of 0.75 for equiprobable bits but no transitions in runs of ones, which is why clock recovery matters.
Signals & systems

Transform-Based Audio Manipulation

Signals and SystemsDec 2024With Sami N. Khalil and Youssef K. Chalhoub

A MATLAB application that manipulates music with Fourier and Z-transform methods, built around an interactive interface.

  • Component separation. FFT-based filtering splits a mixed track into drums (low-pass), vocals (band-pass) and cymbals (high-pass), with cut-offs found by iterative listening and spectrum inspection.
  • Equalization. Bass (0–300 Hz), mid (300–4000 Hz) and treble bands are amplified or attenuated in the frequency domain, then returned to time with an inverse FFT.
  • Effects. Speed change by resampling, reverse playback by reversing samples, and echo through a Z-transform difference equation, plus masking, panning and amplification transformations.
